A little pink on the toothbrush is so common that many people think it is normal. It isn't. Healthy gums do not bleed when you brush or floss, so bleeding is your mouth's way of saying the gums are inflamed.

## What is going on

Plaque, the soft film of bacteria that forms on teeth every day, collects most along the gum line. If it is not removed, the gum reacts: it swells slightly, turns redder and bleeds easily. Dentists call this first stage **gingivitis**. The encouraging part: it can be fully reversed.

When plaque stays for longer it hardens into tartar, which a brush cannot shift. The inflammation can then move below the gum line and start to affect the bone that holds the tooth. This later stage is **periodontitis**. It usually develops quietly, without pain, over months or years.

## Other things that make bleeding worse

- Scrubbing hard, or using a stiff toothbrush
- Hormonal changes, for example during pregnancy
- Diabetes that is not well controlled
- Smoking, which can hide bleeding while the disease continues
- Some medicines, such as blood thinners

## What you can do today

1. Brush twice a day with a **soft** brush, angled towards the gum line.
2. Once a day, clear the gaps between your teeth using floss or tiny interdental brushes.
3. Keep going even if the gums bleed at first: in mild cases bleeding often reduces within a week or two of better cleaning.

## When to see a specialist

Book a gum check if bleeding continues for more than two weeks, or if you notice any of these:

- Gums that look swollen or are shrinking back from the teeth
- Teeth that feel loose or have started to drift apart
- Bad breath that does not go away
- Pus or a bad taste around a tooth
